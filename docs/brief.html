01
The measurement that comes before everything

Can the robot turn around at all?

A two-wheel robot pivots about the middle of its wheel axle, and the corners of the body sweep a circle. That circle has to fit between the walls, and it is the widest the robot ever gets.

Your robot is 250 × 150 mm, so it sweeps 292 mm. The passages are 300 mm. That is 4 mm of clearance each side — geometrically possible, practically not. In simulation it grinds against the walls about 100 times per run.

All three maps, both wall-following directions, 2 seeds each. Target about 21 × 13 cm.

Four ways to shrink it, all free

  • Centre the wheel axle. An axle mounted two-thirds of the way back makes the circle bigger for no benefit. It is just which mounting holes you use.
  • Nothing may stick out. No sensor, bracket, wire loom or battery past the tyres, the nose or the tail. The ultrasonic bracket in particular wants to hang off the front — do not let it.
  • Stack the battery on top, not behind. Anything behind the axle adds to the tail, and the tail is what sweeps.
  • Pull the sensors inboard. The side ultrasonics can sit just inside the wheel line and still see the wall.
Why it is worth the trouble

Every millimetre off the diagonal is a millimetre of margin at every corner of every map. Going from 25 × 15 to 19 × 13 cm takes maps solved from 56% to 75% with no code change at all.

02
Which program to simulate with

The simulator runs the firmware itself

You asked for a simulator good enough that you assemble once and go. Here is the honest version: simulation gets you about 90% of the way, and the last 10% costs one afternoon on a practice track. No simulator knows how your carpet grips.

What it does do is remove the seam where student robot projects usually fail — the tested model drifting away from the shipped code. nav_core.cpp holds all the navigation and no Arduino code at all, so it compiles twice: once by the Arduino IDE onto the Uno, once by g++ into a library the simulator drives. What you watch is what you flash.

Webots

Free, proper 3D physics, has differential drive and IMUs built in. But controllers use its own API, so you would be testing a second, different program. Right next step after this competition, not during it.

Tinkercad Circuits

Fine for showing a teammate how an H-bridge works. Too limited for three sensors and an IMU.

Gazebo / ROS 2, CoppeliaSim, Simulink

All excellent, all far too much machinery for an Arduino Uno with three ultrasonics. Skip them.

Why this changes your strategy

§6.1 awards a point per sector line all three wheels cross, and time is only a tie-breaker. Finishing is not required to score. A robot that never jams and keeps crossing lines beats one that solves two maps perfectly and wedges on the third.

The one shape wall-following cannot solve

If a finish is a chamber in the middle, enclosed by its own ring of wall — which is what photo 1 looks like — then no wall-following robot can ever reach it. The goal is not connected to the wall you are holding. That is maze theory, not a bug.

Our memory mode still collects 5–6 of that map's 7 sector points. And if the real map 1 has its finish gate on the outer edge, as the striped marking suggests, the problem disappears — hence question 2 in the email.

04
Two questions you asked

The gyro, and the memory you already own

Direction sensor — yes, buy two

An MPU-6050 is a sensor, not a microcontroller, so §2.2 explicitly permits it ("use of multiple sensors"). What it buys you:

  • Turns that are actually 90°. Without it you run the motors in opposite directions for 400 ms and hope — wrong by 5–15° every time, and it depends on battery voltage and floor friction. Four corners in, the robot is 40° crooked and wedges.
  • Straight lines. Your two TT motors are not matched; in simulation the right one runs about 6% weak. Without a heading reference the robot curves into a wall down a long corridor.
  • Turning in a doorway, where there are no side walls to reference and the gyro is the only thing that knows which way you point.
The catch, and the fix

Naive integration drifts 15–20° per run, which is exactly enough to jam the robot in the next corner — we measured 22° of error after 50 seconds. The firmware re-measures the gyro's zero-rate bias every time the robot stops (which it does for a quarter-second before every turn), and re-zeroes the heading to the corridor after every completed turn. The gyro then only has to stay honest for the 1.5 s that one turn takes.

Memory module — you don't need one

The ATmega328P on your Uno already contains both kinds of memory, and EEPROM.h ships with the IDE.

MemorySizeWe use
SRAM (working)2048 B192 B
EEPROM (keeps its contents)1024 B192 B

The junction map costs 4 bytes per junction — position plus two bits per compass direction recording how often that exit was used. 48 junctions fit in 192 bytes; a maze this size has maybe 10–20. Nothing to buy, nothing to mount, no rule to worry about.

That memory is what runs Trémaux's algorithm: at each junction take the exit you have used least, breaking ties in wall-following order. It beats plain wall-following on maps with loops — 9.8 versus 8.0 sector points on the serpentine.

The rule that saves runs

At the first junction, the passage behind you is the start gate, so it gets marked "fully used" immediately. Without that, a wall-following robot laps the outer ring and calmly drives back out of its own front door — the most common way to score nothing at this kind of event.

If the budget gets tight

The three you cannot cut are the MPU-6050, the multimeter, and the practice track materials. You cannot tune a maze robot without a maze, and every hour on a real track is worth ten hours of guessing.

Do not buy — §2.2 is a disqualification

No ESP32, Raspberry Pi, Nano or any second microcontroller — not even unused on the board. No external EEPROM or SD module (§4 explains why). And skip the magnetometer compass: it reads the motor magnets and lies to you. Use the green perfboard instead of the white breadboard — spring contacts shake loose, and §5.1 checks for exactly that.

06
Ordered so nothing gets built before it's needed

The plan

  1. Phase 0
    1 hour

    Before you spend a riyal

    Send the email. Measure your robot — length, width, wheelbase, wheel diameter, nose-to-axle — put the numbers in config.h, run tools/geometry_check.py, then watch the maps drive.

  2. Phase 1
    2 days

    Buy and build — practice track first

    Build the 2 m track before you touch the robot; it is the thing you will be tempted to skip and the thing that decides the result. Then assemble with the axle centred, solder the perfboard, wire it, and re-measure where the sensors actually ended up.

  3. Phase 2
    1 day

    Bring it up, one subsystem at a time

    Motors, then encoders, then sonars, then gyro, then line sensor — each a throwaway sketch with a pass condition. Debugging three subsystems at once is how a week disappears.

  4. Phase 3
    1 day

    Tune on the track

    Straight corridor, then one corner, then a dead end, then each map shape ten times. Every change goes into config.h and gets re-checked in the simulator before it goes on the robot. Log the robot's telemetry and compare it to the simulator's — where they disagree tells you which model parameter is wrong, and after you fix it the simulator predicts your robot.

  5. Phase 4
    half a day

    Reliability, not speed

    Ten clean runs per map shape. Start it 30 mm off-centre and 10° crooked, because the judges will not place it perfectly. Run it on half-flat batteries. Then lower the cruise speed until the failure rate hits zero and leave it there.

  6. Phase 5
    the day

    Measure the real maze, then choose your hand

    Trace each map into the simulator on arrival — fifteen minutes each — and it tells you which wall-following direction wins before your run. The robot lets you pick at the start line with a hand gesture: a quick tap in front of the nose is left-hand, a long hold is right-hand, and the countdown blinks slow or fast to confirm. No laptop, no radio, nothing to touch.

07
Found in simulation, before any hardware existed

Four bugs that would have cost you the event

The steering was inverted

The wall-centring term had the wrong sign, so the robot steered into whichever wall it was already too close to. Fixing it cut wall scrapes from about 2,500 per run to under 100.

It turned into a wall in the first two seconds

Junction detection asked "is the side open right now?", which is true the instant the robot rolls out of the gate into the open start zone — before it has seen a single wall. It now triggers on the edge from wall to open, debounced over nine loops so one dropped ping cannot invent a junction.

It pivoted from the wrong place

The ultrasonic sits 95 mm ahead of the wheel axle. Stop when the sensor sees the wall and the axle is still 95 mm short of the junction centre — pivot from there and the robot lands 95 mm off the new corridor's centreline, which in a 300 mm passage means hitting the wall. It now creeps until the axle is in the middle, ranging off the wall ahead.

Every turn leaked a few millimetres

The two wheels are never matched, so during a pivot the odometry gained a few millimetres of phantom travel. Forty turns later the robot thought it was a quarter metre from where it was, and the junction memory became worthless. Translation is now discarded while pivoting.
